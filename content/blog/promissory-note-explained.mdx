A promissory note is one of the most fundamental documents in personal and business finance — and one of the most commonly signed without being fully understood. Whether you're borrowing money from a family member, taking out a private loan, or signing paperwork for a student loan, the promissory note is the document that creates the legal obligation.

Unlike a loan agreement (which can be very long and complex), a promissory note can be just one or two pages. Its simplicity is deceptive. Every element carries legal weight.

## The Elements of a Valid Promissory Note

For a promissory note to be legally enforceable, it must contain certain core elements:

### 1. Principal Amount
The specific dollar amount being borrowed. This is stated clearly at the top of the note. The borrower promises to repay this amount — plus any interest.

### 2. Interest Rate
The cost of borrowing, expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR). This can be:
- **Fixed**: The same rate throughout the loan term (simpler to calculate, more predictable)
- **Variable**: Tied to an index (like the prime rate or SOFR) and can change over time

**Important**: Even between friends and family, the IRS requires a minimum interest rate on loans above $10,000 (called the Applicable Federal Rate, or AFR). If the note doesn't include adequate interest, the IRS may treat the loan as a gift.

### 3. Maturity Date
The date by which the full outstanding balance must be repaid. This is distinct from the payment schedule — a note might have monthly payments for several years, with the final payment or "balloon payment" due on the maturity date.

### 4. Payment Schedule
When payments are due (monthly, quarterly, upon demand), how much each payment is, and how payments are applied (typically first to interest, then to principal — this is called "interest-first amortization").

### 5. Maker and Payee Information
The note identifies who is borrowing (the maker) and who is lending (the payee or holder). Both parties should be clearly identified with full legal names and addresses.

### 6. Signatures
The maker must sign the note. The payee typically doesn't need to sign for it to be binding on the maker, but co-signers (guarantors) must sign if they're accepting personal liability.

## Secured vs. Unsecured Promissory Notes

This distinction is one of the most important in the document.

### Unsecured Promissory Note
The borrower promises to repay based on their general creditworthiness alone. There's no specific asset pledged as collateral. If the borrower defaults, the lender must sue, obtain a judgment, and then pursue collection (wage garnishment, bank levy) — a lengthy and expensive process with no guarantee of recovery.

Most personal loans between individuals are unsecured.

### Secured Promissory Note
The borrower pledges a specific asset — a car, real estate, business equipment — as collateral. If the borrower defaults, the lender can seize and sell the collateral without going through the full court process (depending on the state and the asset type). A mortgage is essentially a promissory note secured by real estate.

When a promissory note is secured, there is typically a separate security agreement or deed of trust that documents the collateral. The note itself may reference this security agreement.

**Key question when reviewing a promissory note**: Does it reference collateral or a security agreement? If so, you're signing a secured note — defaulting means you could lose that specific asset.

## Demand Notes vs. Installment Notes

### Demand Promissory Note
Repayment is due whenever the lender demands it — there's no fixed schedule. The lender can call the loan at any time by giving notice (usually a specified number of days). These are common in informal family lending arrangements but carry significant risk for the borrower.

If you sign a demand note, understand that the lender could require full repayment at any time, even if your financial situation hasn't changed.

### Installment Promissory Note
The most common type for consumer and business loans. Repayment is scheduled in regular installments (monthly, quarterly) over a defined period. Student loans, car loans, and mortgages are all installment notes.

## What Happens If You Default

Default occurs when you miss a payment, miss a certain number of payments, or violate another term of the note (called a covenant). The consequences:

1. **Acceleration clause**: Most promissory notes contain an acceleration clause — if you default, the entire remaining balance becomes immediately due, not just the missed payments. The lender doesn't have to wait for each payment to come due.

2. **Late fees**: Most notes specify a late fee (often 5% of the missed payment or a flat fee) charged when payment is more than a few days late.

3. **Default interest rate**: Some notes include a higher interest rate that kicks in upon default.

4. **Collection action**: For unsecured notes, the lender sues for the accelerated balance. For secured notes, the lender may also repossess or foreclose on the collateral.

5. **Credit reporting**: If the lender reports to credit bureaus (most institutional lenders do; individuals often don't), the default will appear on your credit report.

## Can a Promissory Note Be Sold to a Third Party?

Yes — and this is important to understand. A promissory note is a negotiable instrument, which means the original lender can sell or transfer it to another party (called a "holder in due course"). This happens constantly in the mortgage and student loan industries.

If your note is sold:
- The new holder has the same right to collect as the original lender
- You must receive notice of the transfer and new payment instructions
- Your defenses against the original lender may not apply against a holder in due course (this is a complex legal area — consult an attorney if you're disputing a transferred note)

## Statute of Limitations on Promissory Notes

A lender has a limited window to sue for unpaid amounts on a promissory note. The clock typically starts running when you default. Limits vary by state:

- **3 years**: Many states (California for oral agreements)
- **5–6 years**: Most states for written notes (California for written contracts is 4 years)
- **6 years**: New York, Massachusetts, others

After the statute expires, the lender can't successfully sue to collect (though the debt technically still exists). This is important for old private debts — not federal student loans, which have different rules.

## Before You Sign

Questions to answer before signing any promissory note:
- What is the exact APR, including all fees?
- Is there a prepayment penalty if I pay early?
- What constitutes a default, and what's the cure period?
- Does the acceleration clause apply after one missed payment or multiple?
- Is this note secured, and if so, by what asset?
- Can this note be sold or transferred to another party?
- What is the late payment fee?
