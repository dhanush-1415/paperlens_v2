Medical bills are among the most confusing documents most Americans will ever receive. They're filled with billing codes, unexplained charges, and amounts that often have nothing to do with what you should actually pay. Here's how to decode yours.

## Wait Before You Pay

The most important thing to do when a medical bill arrives: **don't pay it immediately**. Wait until:

1. Your insurance company has processed the claim
2. You've received and reviewed the Explanation of Benefits (EOB)
3. You've confirmed the bill matches the EOB

Paying before your EOB arrives can mean paying more than you owe — and getting a refund from a hospital is notoriously difficult.

## Key Terms on a Medical Bill

**Statement Date**: When the bill was generated. Payment due dates are typically 30 days from this date.

**Account Number**: Your reference number for this billing account. Always include it in correspondence.

**Patient vs. Guarantor**: The patient is who received care; the guarantor is who's responsible for payment (often the same person, or a parent for a minor).

**Amount Billed / Gross Charges**: The full list-price total. This is not what you owe. It's rarely what anyone pays.

**Insurance Payments**: What your insurer has already paid.

**Adjustments / Write-offs**: Contractual discounts applied because of your insurance contract or financial assistance program.

**Patient Responsibility**: What you actually owe after insurance and adjustments. This should match your EOB.

**Itemized Charges**: A line-by-line breakdown. You must request this — it's not always included by default.

## Always Request an Itemized Bill

The summary bill that arrives in the mail tells you the total. It doesn't show you the specific charges. Request an itemized statement (you're legally entitled to one) and review every line.

Common line items include:
- **Room and board** (for inpatient stays) — charged per day
- **Nursing services**
- **Lab tests** — each test billed separately with a CPT code
- **Medications** — each drug administered, often at marked-up prices
- **Medical supplies** — gloves, gowns, IV bags
- **Radiology** — X-rays, CT scans, MRIs
- **Physician services** — often billed separately by the doctor, not the facility

## Common Billing Errors to Look For

**Duplicate charges**: The same service billed twice. Look for identical CPT codes and dates.

**Upcoding**: A simpler procedure billed as a more complex (and expensive) one. Example: a standard office visit billed as a complex consultation.

**Unbundling**: Services that should be billed together as a package are billed separately to increase total charges.

**Services not rendered**: Items billed that you didn't receive — sometimes due to template errors in billing software.

**Wrong patient**: Another patient's charges mixed into your bill (more common than you'd think).

**Non-covered services billed as covered**: The provider assumed something was covered and billed your insurance; if denied, you shouldn't automatically pay the full charge.

**Admission date errors**: Wrong date of service can affect insurance coverage, especially for services requiring prior authorization.

## How to Dispute a Medical Bill

### Step 1: Get the Itemized Bill and EOB
Request both and lay them side by side. The EOB shows what your insurer agreed to pay and what you should owe. The itemized bill shows what the provider charged.

### Step 2: Identify Discrepancies
- Does the bill total match the "patient responsibility" on your EOB?
- Are there charges on the itemized bill you don't recognize?
- Are there services dated on days you weren't at the facility?

### Step 3: Contact the Provider's Billing Department
Call (and follow up in writing). Explain the specific discrepancy. Ask them to:
- Correct coding errors
- Resubmit to your insurance with corrections
- Remove charges for services you didn't receive

### Step 4: Negotiate
Even if the bill is correct, medical bills are often negotiable:
- **Uninsured / self-pay discount**: Most hospitals offer 30–50% discounts to uninsured patients who ask
- **Income-based financial assistance**: Many nonprofit hospitals are required to offer charity care
- **Payment plans**: Nearly all providers offer them; ask for 0% interest

### Step 5: Medical Billing Advocate
For large, complex bills, a professional medical billing advocate can often negotiate significant reductions. Many work on contingency — they take a percentage of what they save you.

## The 72-Hour Rule

Hospitals often charge room and board for your departure day as if it's a full day. If you were discharged in the morning, you shouldn't be billed for a full day. Request a review of any departure-day charges over a half-day.

## What to Do About Surprise Bills

The **No Surprises Act** (effective 2022) protects you from unexpected out-of-network bills in most cases involving:
- Emergency services
- Services from out-of-network providers at in-network facilities (like an anesthesiologist you didn't choose)

If you receive a bill like this, file a complaint with your insurer and the federal No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059).
