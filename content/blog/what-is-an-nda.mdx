Non-Disclosure Agreements show up in job offers, business negotiations, freelance contracts, and investment conversations. Most people sign them without reading them carefully — and occasionally regret it. Here's how to understand one before you sign.

## What Is an NDA?

A Non-Disclosure Agreement (also called a confidentiality agreement) is a legal contract in which one or more parties agree to keep specific information secret and not share it with others. The information is typically described as "confidential information" and defined in the agreement.

NDAs serve legitimate purposes:
- Protecting trade secrets during business discussions
- Keeping proprietary processes confidential with employees
- Securing patent-pending inventions before public disclosure
- Confidential negotiations between companies or investors

## Two Main Types of NDAs

### One-Way (Unilateral) NDA
Only one party discloses information; the other party agrees to keep it confidential. Common in employer-employee relationships and when a company shares sensitive information with a vendor.

### Mutual (Bilateral) NDA
Both parties disclose and both agree to keep each other's information confidential. Common in merger discussions, joint ventures, and equal-footing business negotiations.

## Core Elements of Every NDA

### 1. Definition of Confidential Information
This is the most critical clause. It defines exactly what information is protected. Look for:

- **Broad definitions**: Some NDAs define confidential information as "any information shared, regardless of how labeled" — this is very expansive
- **Narrow definitions**: Specifically listed categories (financial data, customer lists, source code, formulas)
- **Exclusions**: Most NDAs exclude information that is already publicly known, independently developed, or received from a third party without restriction

**What to watch**: An overly broad definition can prevent you from using skills and knowledge you developed before the relationship or information that becomes public through other means.

### 2. Obligations of the Receiving Party
What you must do (and not do) with the information:
- Maintain confidentiality (use the same level of protection as your own confidential info, at minimum)
- Not disclose to unauthorized third parties
- Use the information only for the stated purpose
- Notify the disclosing party of any unauthorized disclosure

### 3. Term (Duration)
How long the NDA lasts. Common structures:
- **During the relationship only**: Ends when you stop working together
- **Fixed term after agreement**: "3 years from date of signing"
- **Indefinitely for certain categories**: Trade secrets may be protected indefinitely under law even without a specific NDA provision

**What to watch**: A 5+ year NDA in a fast-moving industry may be unreasonable and unenforceable. Courts look at whether the duration is reasonable.

### 4. Exclusions and Exceptions
Standard NDAs don't prevent you from:
- Disclosing information already in the public domain
- Disclosing information you already knew before the NDA
- Disclosing information independently developed without using the confidential info
- Complying with a court order or legal process (required by law)
- Disclosing to attorneys or accountants who need it to advise you

If these exclusions aren't in the NDA, they generally still apply — but it's better to have them explicit.

### 5. Non-Compete and Non-Solicitation Clauses (The Hidden Gotchas)
Some NDAs bundle in additional restrictions:
- **Non-compete**: You can't work for competitors for X years in X geography
- **Non-solicitation (employees)**: You can't recruit the company's employees
- **Non-solicitation (clients)**: You can't reach out to their customers

**These are separate from confidentiality** but often snuck into NDAs. Non-competes are heavily regulated by state law — California doesn't enforce them at all; other states limit duration and geography. Review these clauses especially carefully.

### 6. Remedies
What happens if you breach the NDA:
- **Injunctive relief**: The other party can get a court order stopping your disclosure immediately (no need to prove damages)
- **Liquidated damages**: A preset amount you owe for any breach
- **Legal fees**: You pay their attorney's fees if they sue and win

**What to watch**: Liquidated damages clauses should be proportional to actual likely harm — not a punishing penalty.

## Red Flags in NDAs

- **Overly broad "confidential information" definition** with no meaningful exclusions
- **No expiration date** or very long duration
- **Non-compete restrictions** buried in confidentiality language
- **Unreasonable geographic scope** (e.g., "worldwide" for a local business)
- **No mutual reciprocity** when the relationship involves sharing from both sides
- **Personal liability** — signing as an individual when you intended to sign as a business entity

## Employee NDAs

Employers routinely ask new employees to sign NDAs as a condition of employment. In most states this is legal. Key considerations:

- NDAs cannot prevent you from discussing wages, working conditions, or workplace harassment/discrimination with coworkers (protected by the National Labor Relations Act)
- NDAs cannot bind you to trade secrets you didn't actually learn or use
- NDAs about prior employers — you cannot disclose your previous employer's trade secrets, even if the new employer asks
