A Notice of Default (NOD) is a formal document recorded by your lender with the county government declaring that you've fallen behind on your mortgage payments. It's not a foreclosure — but it's the official start of the foreclosure timeline. Here's what you're facing and what you can still do.

## What Is a Notice of Default?

When you miss multiple mortgage payments (typically 3–6 months depending on the lender and state), your lender records a Notice of Default with the county recorder's office. This public document:

- Formally declares you are in default on your loan
- States the amount past due
- Begins the legal foreclosure timeline
- Becomes a public record (affecting your credit and title)

## What Triggers a Notice of Default?

Lenders are generally required to wait at least **120 days** after you first miss a payment before filing a NOD (federal mortgage servicing rules). During this time, they should attempt to contact you and offer loss mitigation options.

After 120 days, if no resolution is reached, the lender can record the NOD and start the foreclosure clock.

## The Foreclosure Timeline After an NOD

The timeline varies significantly by state and whether your state uses judicial or non-judicial foreclosure:

### Non-Judicial Foreclosure States (faster)

California, Texas, Arizona, Nevada, Oregon, Washington, Colorado:

1. **Notice of Default recorded** — clock starts
2. **3-month waiting period** (most states)
3. **Notice of Sale posted** — auction date announced (typically 20–30 days out)
4. **Trustee's Sale** — property sold at auction
5. **Eviction** — if you haven't vacated

In California, the entire process from NOD to sale can take as little as **4–5 months**.

### Judicial Foreclosure States (slower)

New York, New Jersey, Florida, Illinois, Ohio:

1. **Notice of Default / missed payments**
2. **Lender files lawsuit** in court
3. **You are served** and have time to respond (typically 20–30 days)
4. **Court proceedings** — can take 1–3+ years
5. **Judgment** and sale ordered
6. **Sheriff's sale**

You have significantly more time in judicial states, but also more legal complexity.

## Your Options After Receiving a Notice of Default

### 1. Reinstatement (Pay What You Owe)

You can stop the foreclosure entirely by paying all missed payments, late fees, and attorney costs in a lump sum. This is called "reinstating" the loan.

Most states allow reinstatement up to 5 business days before the scheduled sale date. If you have family support, a loan, or savings you can access — this is the cleanest solution.

### 2. Loan Modification

Contact your lender's loss mitigation department immediately. A loan modification changes the terms of your loan — extending the term, reducing the interest rate, or adding missed payments to the back end of the loan (capitalization).

**Apply as early as possible** — lenders cannot proceed with a foreclosure sale while a complete modification application is under review (under federal rules known as "dual tracking" protections).

### 3. Forbearance Agreement

Your lender temporarily reduces or suspends your payments, then creates a repayment plan once your hardship passes. This is common for temporary income disruptions (job loss, medical emergency, natural disaster).

### 4. Short Sale

If you owe more than the home is worth, you can sell the property for less than the outstanding loan balance — with lender approval. A short sale avoids foreclosure on your record and typically causes less credit damage.

### 5. Deed in Lieu of Foreclosure

You voluntarily transfer the property to the lender in exchange for releasing you from the mortgage debt. The lender avoids the cost of foreclosure; you avoid the foreclosure on your record.

### 6. Bankruptcy (Automatic Stay)

Filing Chapter 13 bankruptcy immediately stops the foreclosure (via the automatic stay) and allows you to propose a repayment plan to catch up on mortgage arrears over 3–5 years while keeping the home. Chapter 7 only delays foreclosure temporarily.

### 7. Contest the Foreclosure

If the lender failed to follow proper procedures (improper notice, failure to offer loss mitigation, dual tracking violations), you may have grounds to challenge the foreclosure in court.

## Get Help Immediately

Free foreclosure counseling is available through:

- **HUD-approved housing counselors**: Find one at consumerfinance.gov or call 800-569-4287
- **State housing agencies**: Many offer direct assistance programs
- **Legal aid organizations**: Free legal representation for qualifying homeowners

Do not wait. The options available at the NOD stage are far better than anything available after a Notice of Sale is posted.
