You received a cease and desist letter. Your name is on it. An attorney's letterhead is at the top. Before you spiral, understand the most important thing: **a cease and desist is not a lawsuit**. It's a formal demand. What you do next depends entirely on who sent it and what they're demanding.

## What a Cease and Desist Letter Actually Is

A cease and desist (C&D) letter is a written demand that you stop doing something — immediately and permanently. It is sent by:
- An attorney on behalf of a client
- A debt collector (under specific federal rules)
- A company's in-house legal team
- An individual acting on their own (rare, but happens)

The letter typically states:
1. What you're accused of doing
2. Why the sender believes it's unlawful or harmful to them
3. A demand that you stop immediately
4. A deadline to respond
5. A threat of legal action if you don't comply

Key point: **No court has reviewed this.** A C&D carries no legal authority on its own. The sender cannot force you to do anything based on the letter alone. Only a court can do that. But ignoring certain types of C&D letters creates serious downstream risk.

## Who Sends Cease and Desist Letters

### Attorneys on Behalf of Businesses or Individuals
This is the most common type. A company believes you're infringing their trademark, a neighbor believes you're encroaching on their property, an ex-business partner believes you're violating a non-compete. The attorney sends a formal demand before deciding whether to file suit.

**Seriousness level**: High. An attorney has been paid to write this letter. The client is paying for legal advice and has been told they have a viable claim. Don't dismiss these.

### Debt Collectors
Under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA), you have the right to send a debt collector a written cease and desist demanding they stop contacting you. But you can also receive a C&D from a creditor's attorney.

**Seriousness level**: Moderate. The debt doesn't disappear when you send a C&D — the collector must stop contacting you but can still sue you to collect.

### Intellectual Property Owners
A business or individual believes you're using their trademark, copyright, or trade secret. These letters are common in e-commerce, content creation, and software.

**Seriousness level**: High. IP law is complex and enforcement can be expensive for both sides. Take these seriously.

### Former Employers
Alleging you violated a non-compete, non-solicitation agreement, or took confidential information.

**Seriousness level**: High. Non-compete enforcement has become more limited in some states, but these claims still result in injunctions and damages suits.

## When It's Serious vs. When It's Bluster

### Signs the Letter Is Serious
- Sent by a named attorney at a real law firm (verify this — look up the attorney and firm)
- Specific, documented claims with dates, screenshots, or references to legal code
- A clear deadline with specific consequences stated
- The claiming party has a history of litigation or the resources to sue
- The claim involves significant money or intellectual property

### Signs It May Be Bluster
- Sent directly by the individual (no attorney)
- Vague or exaggerated claims with no legal citations
- Sent by a company or person with no apparent resources or track record of litigation
- The demand is for something you have a clear legal right to do

Even if you believe a letter is bluster, **never put that assessment in writing** in any reply to the sender.

## What You Must NEVER Do

### Never Ignore a Letter from an Attorney

Attorney-sent C&D letters have a response expectation. If you ignore it, the natural next step for the attorney is to advise their client to file suit. You don't want to be the person who made that decision easy by not responding at all.

### Never Admit to the Allegations in Writing

Do not send an email, text, or letter saying "you're right, I'll stop." Admissions can be used in litigation. A simple acknowledgment that you received the letter and are reviewing it is sufficient.

### Never Make Threats in Response

Sending an angry reply threatening counter-action — especially without legal advice — often makes things worse.

### Never Destroy Evidence

If litigation is possible, destroying documents, emails, or files related to the claim is spoliation. That can result in sanctions and adverse inferences in court.

## How to Respond

**Step 1: Verify the sender.** Look up the attorney's bar number. Confirm the law firm exists. Confirm the company or person they represent is real.

**Step 2: Read the claims carefully.** What specific action are they demanding you stop? Is it something you actually did? Is it something you have a legal right to do?

**Step 3: Consult an attorney before responding.** If the C&D is from an attorney, you should have your own attorney review and respond. This is not optional for high-stakes claims. Many attorneys offer a free or low-cost initial consultation.

**Step 4: Respond by the deadline — or ask for more time.** If you need more time to consult an attorney, send a brief acknowledgment that you received the letter and are reviewing it with counsel. This is standard practice and buys you time without admitting anything.

**Step 5: Evaluate your options.** Options typically include: comply fully, comply partially, dispute the claims, negotiate a resolution, or do nothing and wait to see if they file suit.

## Cease and Desist from Debt Collectors: Your Rights

If you want to stop a debt collector from contacting you, you can send them a written C&D. Under the FDCPA, once they receive it, they can only contact you to confirm they've stopped or to notify you they intend to take legal action.

Your C&D does not erase the debt. They can still sue you. But it gives you control over the contact.

## The Bottom Line

A cease and desist letter is a serious document that deserves a thoughtful response — not panic, and not dismissal. Read it carefully, verify who sent it, and get legal advice before responding to any attorney-sent letter. Time matters. Most C&D letters have response deadlines that, if missed, accelerate the path to litigation.
