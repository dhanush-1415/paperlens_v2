If you owe the IRS money but genuinely cannot pay without going hungry or losing your housing, there is a legal option the IRS rarely advertises: **Currently Not Collectible (CNC) status**. Once granted, the IRS stops all active collection — no levies, no garnishments, no seizures. But it comes with conditions you need to understand before you apply.

## What "Currently Not Collectible" Actually Means

CNC is not forgiveness. The IRS is not erasing your debt. They are temporarily agreeing that collecting from you right now would cause you **economic hardship** — meaning your allowable living expenses exceed or equal your monthly income.

When CNC status is in effect:

- IRS levies stop (or are released if already active)
- Wage garnishments stop
- Bank account seizures stop
- IRS collection calls stop (for most taxpayers)
- The IRS will not file a tax lien while you are in CNC (though an existing lien remains)

The debt doesn't go away. Interest and penalties continue to accrue every month you're in CNC status.

## How to Qualify

The IRS evaluates CNC eligibility using a financial analysis. They compare your **monthly income** against your **allowable monthly expenses** using their National and Local Standards — fixed amounts for housing, food, transportation, and healthcare based on your family size and location.

You qualify if:

- Your allowable monthly expenses meet or exceed your monthly income
- Your assets have little or no equity (or assets are exempt, like a retirement account)
- Paying the debt would leave you unable to meet basic living expenses

Common situations where CNC is granted:

- Fixed-income retirees living on Social Security alone
- Individuals receiving disability income with high medical expenses
- Unemployed taxpayers with no significant assets
- People in bankruptcy proceedings

## How to Request CNC Status

### Option 1: Call the IRS Directly

Call the IRS Collections line at **1-800-829-1040**. Tell them you are experiencing financial hardship and request Currently Not Collectible status. Be prepared to provide your monthly income, expenses, and asset information verbally.

Have these numbers ready:

- Monthly gross income (wages, benefits, rental income, etc.)
- Monthly housing costs (rent or mortgage + utilities)
- Monthly transportation costs (car payment + insurance + operating costs)
- Monthly food and household expenses
- Monthly healthcare and insurance costs
- Any other necessary expenses

### Option 2: Submit Form 433-F or 433-A

For larger balances, the IRS may require a written Collection Information Statement:

- **Form 433-F**: Used for most individual taxpayers — simpler, one page
- **Form 433-A**: More detailed, used for self-employed individuals or complex finances

These forms require documentation: bank statements, pay stubs, utility bills, mortgage statements. Gather these before submitting.

If you owe more than $100,000, you'll almost certainly need to submit a written form and may want professional help from an enrolled agent or tax attorney.

## What Happens While You're in CNC Status

### The Statute of Limitations Keeps Running

The IRS has **10 years from the date of assessment** to collect a tax debt. That clock does not stop during CNC status. This is actually a benefit — time passes, and if you reach the Collection Statute Expiration Date (CSED) while in CNC, the debt expires and cannot be collected.

### Interest and Penalties Keep Accruing

This is the significant downside. While the IRS isn't actively collecting, interest compounds daily and failure-to-pay penalties continue at 0.5% per month. A $10,000 debt today will be a larger debt in three years.

### The IRS Will Review Your Status Annually

The IRS monitors CNC taxpayers. Each year when you file your tax return, the IRS checks your income. If your financial situation improves beyond their thresholds, they will remove CNC status and resume collection activity. You'll receive a notice if that happens.

### You Must Keep Filing Returns

Being in CNC status does not excuse you from filing tax returns each year. If you stop filing, you lose CNC status and the IRS can resume collection immediately. File every year, even if you cannot pay.

### New Tax Debts Can Jeopardize CNC Status

If you create new tax debt (fail to pay taxes in a future year), the IRS may remove your CNC status for the older debt as well. Stay current on new obligations.

## When CNC Status Ends

CNC status ends when:

1. Your income increases above expense thresholds (discovered at annual review)
2. You acquire assets or equity that makes you collectible
3. The CSED expires and the debt disappears entirely
4. You resolve the debt through payment, an Offer in Compromise, or bankruptcy

## CNC vs. Other IRS Relief Options

| Option                    | What It Does              | Best For                                  |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **CNC Status**            | Pauses collection         | Genuinely cannot pay now                  |
| **Installment Agreement** | Monthly payment plan      | Can pay something each month              |
| **Offer in Compromise**   | Settle for less than owed | Permanent inability to pay full amount    |
| **Penalty Abatement**     | Removes penalties         | First-time or reasonable cause situations |

## Getting Professional Help

For balances over $10,000, working with an enrolled agent, CPA, or tax attorney dramatically improves your outcome. They know exactly how to document expenses to meet IRS standards and can negotiate on your behalf if the IRS pushes back on the initial request.

The IRS is not your enemy in this process — they would rather pause collection than deal with a taxpayer who truly cannot pay. CNC exists precisely because the IRS recognizes that collecting from someone with no means to pay costs more than it's worth.
