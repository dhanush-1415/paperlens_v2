Being served with a summons is one of the most jarring things that can happen in ordinary life. It looks official, it has a court seal, and it says you're being sued. Here's what it actually means and, critically, what the deadline says about how long you have to act.

## What Is a Summons?

A summons is an official court document that notifies you that a lawsuit has been filed against you. It is almost always accompanied by a **Complaint** — the document that describes the plaintiff's claims against you.

The summons itself doesn't mean you've lost anything or done anything wrong. It means someone is suing you and a court has officially recognized the lawsuit.

## What Is a Complaint?

The complaint (or petition, in some courts) is the document that explains:
- Who is suing you (the plaintiff)
- What they're claiming you did
- What they're asking the court to award them (damages, injunctions, etc.)
- The legal basis for their claims

Read the complaint carefully. Specifically look for:
- The specific claims (breach of contract, negligence, fraud, etc.)
- The amount of money being sought
- What they want the court to order you to do or stop doing

## What Are Your Deadlines?

This is the most critical piece of information. The summons will state how many days you have to respond — this is called your **response deadline** or **time to answer**.

Typical deadlines:
- **Federal court**: 21 days
- **State courts**: 20–30 days (varies by state)
- **Small claims court**: Often 10–14 days

**These deadlines are absolute.** If you miss your response deadline, the plaintiff can request a **default judgment** — a court ruling in their favor because you didn't respond. Once entered, a default judgment can be enforced like any other judgment: wage garnishment, bank levy, property liens.

## How Service Works

You can only be formally "served" in specific ways recognized by your state's law:
- **Personal service**: A process server or sheriff physically hands you the documents
- **Substitute service**: Left with someone of suitable age at your home or business
- **Service by mail**: Some courts allow this with acknowledgment
- **Publication**: For parties who can't be found — rare and court-approved

If you were not properly served, you may be able to challenge the lawsuit's jurisdiction. However, don't rely on this without consulting an attorney.

## How to Respond to a Summons

### Step 1: Don't Ignore It
This cannot be overstated. Ignoring a summons almost always results in a default judgment against you, which is harder and more expensive to undo than responding in the first place.

### Step 2: Read the Complaint Completely
Understand exactly what you're accused of and what evidence (if any) they're claiming to have.

### Step 3: Calculate Your Deadline
Count the days from when you were served. Mark the response deadline on your calendar immediately.

### Step 4: Decide How to Respond

**Option A: Consult an Attorney**
For anything involving more than a few thousand dollars, a lawsuit involving a contract dispute, personal injury, business matter, or anything where you have a genuine defense — get legal help. Many attorneys offer free consultations.

**Option B: Represent Yourself (Pro Se)**
For small claims or straightforward matters, you can file an Answer yourself. An Answer should:
- Admit or deny each allegation in the complaint paragraph by paragraph
- State any affirmative defenses you have
- Include a certificate of service (confirming you sent a copy to the plaintiff)
- Be filed with the court before the deadline

**Option C: Settle**
Contact the plaintiff or their attorney to explore settlement before your answer deadline. Many lawsuits settle before any court appearance. Filing an answer doesn't prevent settlement.

## Common Types of Lawsuits

**Debt collection lawsuit**: A creditor or debt buyer suing for an unpaid balance. Your defenses may include: the statute of limitations has expired, the debt wasn't yours, the amount is wrong, or you already paid.

**Contract dispute**: Someone claiming you breached a contract. Review the contract carefully — are their claims accurate? Did you have a reason for non-performance?

**Personal injury**: Someone claiming you caused them harm. Do not communicate with them without legal representation.

**Small claims court**: Usually for amounts under $5,000–$10,000. Less formal. You can often represent yourself effectively.

**Landlord-tenant**: Eviction or unpaid rent claims. Respond — even in small claims.

## After You File an Answer

Once you file an Answer, the case proceeds:
1. **Discovery**: Both sides exchange information and evidence
2. **Mediation/settlement**: Many cases settle before trial
3. **Pretrial motions**: Either side may file motions to dismiss claims or limit the case
4. **Trial**: If no settlement, a judge (or jury) decides
5. **Judgment**: The final court ruling

Most cases settle long before trial.
