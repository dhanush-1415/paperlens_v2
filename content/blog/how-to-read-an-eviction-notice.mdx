Receiving an eviction notice is one of the most stressful things a tenant can experience. But before you start packing boxes, understand this: **a notice is not an eviction**. It is the first step in a legal process that has rules — and you have rights throughout it.

## Types of Eviction Notices

The type of notice determines your timeline and options.

### Pay or Quit Notice (3–5 days)
This is the most common notice. You owe rent and have a short window to either **pay the full amount owed** or **vacate**. The number of days varies by state (3 days in California, 5 days in New York, etc.).

If you pay the full amount within the notice period, the eviction process stops. The landlord cannot proceed if you've cured the default.

### Cure or Quit Notice
You've violated a lease term other than nonpayment — a pet, a subletter, damage, noise complaints. You have a set number of days to **fix the violation** or vacate.

### Unconditional Quit Notice
The most serious type. You are being asked to leave with **no option to remedy**. These are typically issued for:
- Repeated lease violations
- Illegal activity on the premises
- Severe property damage
- A second nonpayment in the same period

This notice cannot be resolved by paying rent or fixing a violation. It proceeds directly to court if you don't leave.

### Notice of Termination (Month-to-Month or Fixed-Term)
The landlord is ending the tenancy without cause (where allowed). Notice periods are usually 30, 60, or 90 days depending on state law and tenancy length.

## What to Read in Your Notice

Every eviction notice should contain:

1. **Your name and address** — confirm it's addressed to you
2. **The reason for the notice** — nonpayment, lease violation, or no-cause
3. **The amount owed** (if nonpayment) — verify this matches your records
4. **The deadline** — the exact date you must pay, cure, or vacate
5. **How to respond** — where to send payment or written response
6. **Landlord's signature and date** — required for validity in most states

## When a Notice Is Invalid

Not every notice is legally enforceable. Common defects include:

- **Wrong notice period** — if your state requires 5 days and you were given 3, the notice is defective
- **Incorrect amount** — the landlord cannot include fees that aren't legally owed
- **Improper service** — most states require specific delivery methods (posting on door, certified mail, personal service)
- **Retaliation** — if the notice came within 90 days of a complaint to housing authorities, it may be retaliatory and therefore unenforceable
- **Discriminatory basis** — evictions based on race, family status, disability, etc. violate the Fair Housing Act

If any of these apply, document it and consult a tenant's rights organization or attorney immediately.

## Your Options After Receiving a Notice

### Option 1: Pay (for Pay or Quit notices)
If you owe rent and can pay, pay within the notice period. Get a receipt or send a certified check. The eviction must stop.

### Option 2: Fix the Violation (for Cure or Quit notices)
Remove the unauthorized pet, fix the damage, stop the noise. Document that you've done so in writing to your landlord.

### Option 3: Negotiate with Your Landlord
Landlords often prefer payment over court proceedings. A payment plan, partial payment with a clear commitment, or move-out date agreement can sometimes resolve the matter without court.

### Option 4: Fight It in Court
If the notice is invalid, the amount is wrong, or you have defenses (habitability issues, retaliation, discrimination), you can raise them at the eviction hearing. The landlord must still win in court before you can be removed.

### Option 5: Seek Assistance
Many cities have emergency rental assistance programs. A call to 211 or a local legal aid organization can connect you with resources.

## The Eviction Timeline After a Notice

If you don't respond, the process typically goes:

1. **Notice period expires** (3–90 days depending on type)
2. **Landlord files an eviction (unlawful detainer) lawsuit** in court
3. **You are served with the summons** and have 5–10 days to respond
4. **Court hearing** — usually 2–6 weeks after filing
5. **Judgment** — if the court rules for the landlord, you typically get a short period to vacate (3–7 days in most states)
6. **Writ of possession** — the court authorizes the sheriff to physically remove you if you don't leave

**Important**: A landlord cannot change locks, remove belongings, or cut off utilities without a court order. Self-help eviction is illegal in every state.
