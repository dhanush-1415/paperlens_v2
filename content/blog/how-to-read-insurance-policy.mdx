Insurance policies can run 50–100 pages. Most people never read them — and that's exactly what insurance companies count on. Knowing what your policy covers before you file a claim is worth far more than finding out during one. Here's where to focus your attention.

## Start with the Declarations Page (Dec Page)

The Declarations Page is a 1–2 page summary at the front of every policy. It's the most important quick-read you can do. It shows:

- **Named insured**: Who is covered (make sure your name is spelled correctly and all covered parties are listed)
- **Policy period**: The exact start and end dates of coverage
- **Policy number**: Your reference number for all claims and inquiries
- **Coverage types and limits**: A summary of what you're covered for and how much
- **Deductibles**: What you pay out-of-pocket before insurance kicks in
- **Premiums**: What you're paying (and sometimes the payment schedule)
- **Location/property/vehicle**: What specifically is covered

The Dec page tells you what you have. The rest of the policy tells you exactly how it works.

## The Insuring Agreement: What Is Actually Covered

This is the section where the insurance company promises to pay. Read it carefully — it's often shorter than you'd expect.

The insuring agreement sets the **scope of coverage**: what events (called "perils" in property insurance) are covered. There are two main approaches:

**All-risk / open-perils**: Covers all losses **except** those specifically excluded. Broader coverage.

**Named-perils**: Covers **only** the specific perils listed. If it's not on the list, you're not covered.

**Example**: A standard homeowners policy (HO-3) covers the dwelling on an open-perils basis — all losses except those excluded — but covers personal property on a named-perils basis. This difference matters enormously when a claim arises.

## The Exclusions Section: What's NOT Covered

This is the most important section most people never read.

Common homeowners insurance exclusions:
- **Flooding** (standard policies don't cover floods — you need separate flood insurance through NFIP or private carrier)
- **Earthquakes** (same — separate coverage required)
- **Mold** (unless resulting from a covered peril)
- **Normal wear and tear**
- **Intentional acts**
- **Home business operations** (your home business equipment may not be covered under personal property)
- **Certain dog breeds** (liability coverage often excludes specific high-risk breeds)

Common auto insurance exclusions:
- **Using your personal vehicle for business/rideshare** (if you drive for Uber or deliver food, personal auto may not cover you)
- **Mechanical breakdown** (insurance covers accidents, not breakdowns)
- **Custom equipment** (aftermarket parts often need separate endorsement)

Common health insurance exclusions:
- **Experimental treatments**
- **Cosmetic procedures**
- **Out-of-network providers** (subject to higher cost-sharing or full exclusion)

## Definitions Section: Don't Skip This

Insurance policies define specific terms, and those definitions often matter more than the common meaning of the word.

**"Occurrence" vs. "Claim"**: In liability policies, this determines when coverage triggers — when the event happened (occurrence) or when the claim is filed (claims-made). Critical for long-tail liability like professional liability or malpractice.

**"Bodily injury"**: Carefully defined — may or may not include emotional distress, sickness, or disease depending on context.

**"Residence premises"**: Where the policy applies. Important if you have a secondary home, vacation rental, or are renovating.

**"Business pursuits"**: If the definition excludes business pursuits from personal liability coverage, your home business activities may not be covered.

## Conditions Section: Your Obligations

Insurance is a two-way contract. The conditions section tells you what you must do to maintain coverage and file valid claims:

**Duties after a loss**: Most policies require you to:
- Report the loss promptly
- Protect property from further damage
- Cooperate with the investigation
- Provide a sworn statement (proof of loss)

Failing to meet these duties can give the insurer grounds to deny your claim — even for a valid loss.

**Cancellation and non-renewal**: Under what conditions can the insurer cancel or not renew your policy, and how much notice must they give?

**Subrogation**: After paying your claim, the insurer can pursue the party responsible for your loss in your name. You typically agree not to do anything that impairs this right.

## Endorsements and Riders: Important Add-Ons

Endorsements are modifications to the standard policy — they can add or restrict coverage. Common ones:

- **Scheduled personal property**: Adds coverage for valuables (jewelry, art, instruments) beyond standard limits
- **Home business endorsement**: Extends coverage to business activities at home
- **Water backup endorsement**: Covers sewer/drain backup (often excluded from standard policies)
- **Umbrella policy**: Adds liability coverage above your auto/home limits
- **Actual cash value vs. replacement cost**: A critical endorsement — ACV pays depreciated value; replacement cost pays what it actually costs to replace the item new

Always check if your policy pays ACV or replacement cost. For older roofs and electronics, the difference can be enormous.

## Understanding Coverage Limits and Deductibles

**Per-occurrence limit**: Maximum paid for a single event
**Aggregate limit**: Maximum paid over the policy period
**Per-item limit**: Maximum for a specific category (jewelry, electronics, cash)

**Deductible**: What you pay before insurance pays. Higher deductibles = lower premiums. Make sure your deductible is an amount you can actually access.

**Special deductibles**: Some policies have separate, higher deductibles for specific perils. Hurricane deductibles in coastal states are often 1–5% of the insured value — not a flat dollar amount.
