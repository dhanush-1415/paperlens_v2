A lien sounds abstract until you try to sell your house or refinance your mortgage — and discover one sitting in the title records that nobody told you about. Here's what liens are, which types matter most, and what to do about them.

## What Is a Lien?

A lien is a **legal claim against a specific asset** — usually real property — that secures a debt or obligation. Until the underlying debt is paid, the lienholder has a legal interest in the property. Liens are recorded in county property records and become part of the property's title chain.

The practical consequence: a property with unresolved liens generally **cannot be sold or refinanced** until those liens are satisfied (paid, released, or resolved).

## The Most Common Types of Liens

### 1. Mortgage Lien (Voluntary Lien)

When you take out a mortgage, you grant the lender a lien on your property as collateral. This is the most common lien — it's voluntary, agreed-to, and expected. When you pay off your mortgage, the lender records a lien release.

### 2. Property Tax Lien

Local governments have the right to place a lien on your property if you don't pay property taxes. These liens:

- Are "super-priority" in most states — they take precedence over mortgages
- Accrue interest and penalties
- Can lead to a tax sale (where the government auctions the property) if unpaid for an extended period
- Are automatic — you don't need to be notified for the lien to attach

**Resolution**: Pay the delinquent taxes, plus interest and penalties. The taxing authority will then record a release.

### 3. Federal Tax Lien (IRS Lien)

When you have an unpaid federal tax liability and the IRS has made a demand for payment that you've ignored, the IRS files a **Notice of Federal Tax Lien** in the county where you own property.

This lien:

- Attaches to all property you currently own or will own until the debt is paid
- Is a public record visible to creditors and title companies
- Blocks property sales and refinancing until resolved
- Can be searched by creditors checking your credit

**Resolution options**: Pay in full (lien releases within 30 days), enter a direct debit installment agreement (IRS may withdraw the lien after regular payments), submit an Offer in Compromise, or request a lien discharge on a specific property if you're selling.

### 4. Mechanic's Lien (Construction Lien)

If a contractor, subcontractor, or materials supplier did work on your property and wasn't paid, they can file a mechanic's lien. This is often the most surprising type for homeowners.

You can have a mechanic's lien filed against your property even if:

- You paid the general contractor but they didn't pay their subs
- You dispute the quality of the work
- You didn't directly hire the party filing the lien

**Resolution**: Dispute the lien within the deadline (varies by state — often 90 days), negotiate with the lienholder, or pay the amount. If the lien is invalid, you can file a court action to have it removed.

### 5. Judgment Lien

If a court enters a judgment against you in a civil lawsuit, the winning party can record the judgment as a lien against your real property. Judgment liens:

- Attach to all real property you own in the county of recording
- Remain until paid or the statute of limitations expires (often 10+ years)
- Can be renewed

**Resolution**: Pay the judgment, negotiate a settlement, or in some states, claim exemptions (homestead exemption may protect your primary residence from judgment liens, depending on state law).

### 6. HOA Lien

Homeowners associations can place liens for unpaid dues or fines. Some states give HOAs "super-priority" status — meaning their lien can even trump your mortgage lender.

**Resolution**: Pay the delinquent dues and any late fees. Get a payoff letter and lien release in writing.

## How to Find Out If Your Property Has a Lien

Property liens are public records. You can check:

- **County recorder's office**: Search by your name or property address (usually available online)
- **Title search**: Ordered during a real estate transaction
- **Credit report**: Federal tax liens and judgment liens often appear here

## How to Remove a Lien

The process depends on the type:

1. **Pay the debt**: The most direct path. Get a **lien release** in writing and confirm it's recorded.
2. **Dispute invalid liens**: Mechanic's liens filed without proper notice or past the deadline can be challenged in court.
3. **Negotiate a settlement**: Many lienholders will accept less than the full amount, especially if the debt is old.
4. **Lien subordination**: For the IRS, you can request that a tax lien be moved to a secondary position to allow a refinance (the mortgage lien stays first).
5. **Discharge of lien**: For specific properties — useful if you're selling one piece of property while others remain subject to the lien.

## When You're Buying a Property

Before closing on any real estate purchase, a title search is performed and title insurance is purchased. These protect you from unknown liens. If a lien is discovered:

- The seller must typically resolve it before closing
- Proceeds from the sale can be used to pay it at closing
- Title insurance protects against liens that weren't discovered
