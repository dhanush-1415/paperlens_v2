A CP501 arrives in your mailbox and your stomach drops. **Balance Due** is printed at the top. Before you do anything else, understand what this notice actually is — and what it isn't.

## What Is a CP501?

The CP501 is the IRS's **first automated reminder** that you have an unpaid balance from a prior tax year. It is not a final notice, not a levy warning, and not an audit. It's the IRS saying: "Hey, you owe money — please pay."

The IRS sends CP501 notices when:
- You filed a return but didn't pay the full amount due
- A previous notice (like a CP14) went unanswered
- A payment plan failed or lapsed

## What's Inside the Notice

Every CP501 contains:

1. **Tax year** the balance is from
2. **Amount owed** — including any interest and penalties already accrued
3. **Due date** for payment
4. **Account number** (for referencing when you call or write)
5. **Phone number** for the IRS collections line

The due date on a CP501 is typically **21 days from the notice date**. That date is printed near the top of the letter — not the date you received it.

## Why Interest and Penalties Are Growing

From the day your original balance was due, the IRS charges:

- **Failure-to-pay penalty**: 0.5% of unpaid tax per month (up to 25%)
- **Interest**: Federal short-term rate + 3%, compounded daily

Every month you don't pay, the total grows. A $1,000 balance today is a $1,250+ balance in a year.

## Your Options

### Pay in Full
The cleanest path. Pay online at IRS.gov/payments, by check, or by phone. Make sure to include the tax year and your Social Security number on any check.

### Set Up a Payment Plan (Installment Agreement)
If you can't pay in full, you can apply for a payment plan online in minutes at IRS.gov. A plan stops the failure-to-pay penalty from increasing, though interest continues.

### Apply for Currently Not Collectible (CNC) Status
If paying the balance would prevent you from covering basic living expenses, you may qualify for CNC status. The IRS temporarily halts collection activity, though interest and penalties continue to accrue.

### Offer in Compromise (OIC)
If you genuinely can't pay the full amount, an OIC lets you settle for less. Qualification is strict — the IRS accepts roughly 40% of applications. This takes months and usually requires a tax professional.

## What Happens If You Ignore It

Ignoring a CP501 triggers the escalation sequence:

1. **CP503** — second notice, more urgent tone
2. **CP504** — final notice before levy, certified mail
3. **CP90 / LT11** — Notice of Intent to Levy (you have 30 days to respond before the IRS can seize assets)

Each step adds pressure and limits your options. The time to act is **now**, on the CP501.

## The 3-Step Action Plan

1. **Find the balance and due date** — mark the due date on your calendar immediately
2. **Decide how to pay** — full payment, payment plan, or call a tax professional if the amount is large
3. **Respond by the due date** — even if you can't pay, calling the IRS or setting up a plan prevents the next escalation
