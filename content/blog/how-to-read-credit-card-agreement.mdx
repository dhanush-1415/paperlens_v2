The average American carries 3–4 credit cards. Almost nobody has read their card agreement. These documents determine interest rates, fee structures, dispute rights, and what happens when things go wrong. Here's how to cut through the legalese.

## The Schumer Box: Your Starting Point

Federal law requires credit card issuers to present key terms in a standardized table called the "Schumer Box." Look for it at the beginning of any credit card offer or agreement.

The Schumer Box shows:
- **Purchase APR** (Annual Percentage Rate)
- **Balance Transfer APR**
- **Cash Advance APR**
- **Penalty APR** (what you'll be charged after missing payments)
- **Minimum Interest Charge**
- **Annual Fee**
- **Transaction Fees** (balance transfer, cash advance, foreign transaction)
- **Penalty Fees** (late payment, returned payment)

This is the quick summary. But the agreement itself controls the details.

## Interest Rate Sections

### Purchase APR
The interest rate charged on purchases you don't pay in full each month. Variable APRs are tied to the Prime Rate (a benchmark) plus a margin (e.g., "Prime + 14.99%"). When the Prime Rate rises, your APR rises automatically.

**Grace period**: Most cards give you a 21–25 day grace period after your statement closes to pay in full without incurring interest. If you carry a balance from month to month, you lose the grace period on new purchases — meaning interest accrues from the purchase date.

### Penalty APR
The clause most people never see until it applies to them. If you make a late payment (often defined as more than 60 days late), the issuer can permanently increase your APR on existing and future balances to the penalty rate — often 29.99%.

**CARD Act protection**: The penalty APR can only be applied to new transactions after 6 months of on-time payments, at which point the issuer must reevaluate the rate.

**What triggers it**: Any payment more than 60 days late on this card — or in some cases, late payments on other cards with the same issuer.

### Balance Transfer APR
The rate applied when you move a balance from another card. Often a promotional 0% for 12–21 months, then jumping to the regular purchase APR. The details matter:

- The transfer fee (typically 3–5% of the amount transferred) — this is charged even during 0% promo periods
- What happens to transferred balances when the promo ends
- Whether new purchases during the promo period accrue interest immediately

### Cash Advance APR
Almost always higher than the purchase APR (often 25–30%). More importantly:
- **No grace period** — interest starts the day you take the cash
- **Cash advance fee**: Typically 5% or $10, whichever is greater
- **ATM fees**: Separate fees from your bank or the ATM owner

Using a credit card for cash should be a last resort.

## Fee Sections

### Annual Fee
Stated upfront. Some cards waive it the first year. Know whether the rewards justify the fee.

### Late Payment Fee
The maximum is capped by federal regulation ($30 for first offense, $41 thereafter). Your agreement specifies the exact amount and what counts as "late" (the due date, or a cut-off time on that date — often 5 PM ET).

### Returned Payment Fee
Charged if your autopay bank draft bounces. Also often triggers a late payment fee. Keep your payment account funded.

### Foreign Transaction Fee
Typically 1–3% on purchases made in foreign currencies or from foreign merchants. Many travel cards have eliminated this — check yours.

### Balance Transfer Fee
3–5% of the transferred amount, charged when the transfer posts. This is separate from any promotional APR.

## Payment Application — Where Your Money Goes

This section determines which balances your payments are applied to. Under the CARD Act, payments above the minimum must be applied to the **highest-APR balance first** (good for consumers). But minimum payments can be applied to lower-rate balances — meaning high-APR balances grow.

**Implication**: If you have a 0% promo balance and regular purchase balance, only the amount above your minimum goes to the higher-rate balance. The minimum payment keeps going toward the promo balance.

## Billing Disputes

Your credit card agreement must explain your dispute rights under the Fair Credit Billing Act (FCBA):

- You have **60 days from the statement date** to dispute a charge in writing
- The issuer must investigate and respond within 30 days (and resolve within 2 billing cycles)
- During investigation, you don't have to pay the disputed amount (no interest or late fees on disputed charges)
- If resolved in your favor, charges and related fees are removed
- If resolved against you, you still have the right to explain your position

**Unauthorized charges** (fraud): You are not liable for unauthorized charges if you report them promptly. Zero liability policies are standard on major networks.

## The Arbitration Clause

Many agreements require disputes to go to binding arbitration rather than court. This means:
- You waive your right to sue in court
- You waive your right to participate in class action lawsuits
- An arbitrator (paid by the card company) decides your case

You can often opt out within 30–60 days of receiving the agreement. Look for the opt-out instructions — this is one of the few clauses worth actively managing.

## How to Actually Use This

1. Find the current version of your card agreement (issuers are required to make them available online)
2. Check your current APR and compare to your statement
3. Locate the arbitration clause and evaluate whether to opt out
4. Note what triggers the penalty APR and set up autopay to avoid it
5. Understand the dispute window — 60 days from the statement date, not from when you noticed
