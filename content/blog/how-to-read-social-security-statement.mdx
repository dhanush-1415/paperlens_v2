Most people think of their Social Security statement as something to worry about when they're near retirement. But errors in your earnings record — the data that determines your benefit — are much easier to fix now than later. Here's how to read it and what to check.

## Where to Get Your Statement

The Social Security Administration (SSA) mails paper statements to workers age 60 and older who aren't collecting benefits. For everyone else, you can access yours at **ssa.gov/myaccount** (free SSA account required).

The online statement is updated more frequently than mailed versions and includes more detail.

## Section 1: Your Estimated Benefits

The statement shows estimated monthly benefit amounts at three claiming ages:

### Retirement Benefits
- **Age 62**: The earliest you can claim. Claiming early permanently reduces your benefit — typically by 25–30% compared to full retirement.
- **Full Retirement Age (FRA)**: Your full benefit. FRA is 67 for anyone born 1960 or later; 66 for earlier birth years.
- **Age 70**: The maximum benefit. Waiting until 70 gives you about 24–32% more than claiming at FRA.

**These are estimates in today's dollars.** The actual benefit will be adjusted for inflation between now and when you claim.

### Disability Benefits
Your estimated monthly benefit if you become disabled before retirement and can no longer work. This requires sufficient work credits (see below).

### Survivor Benefits
Estimated amounts your spouse and dependent children would receive if you die. This section is critical if you have dependents.

## Section 2: Your Earnings Record — The Most Important Section

This is a year-by-year list of every dollar of Social Security-taxable earnings ever reported under your Social Security number.

### What to Look For

**Missing years**: If you worked in a particular year and see $0 or a lower-than-expected amount, the employer may have filed your W-2 with incorrect information, or there may be a data entry error at the SSA.

**Wrong name/SSN on W-2**: If your employer used a slightly wrong name or Social Security number on your tax forms, those earnings may not have been credited to your record.

**Self-employment earnings**: If you were self-employed, verify your Schedule SE earnings match what's recorded. These are only credited if you actually filed your taxes.

**Multiple employers**: If you had multiple jobs in one year, the total should reflect combined wages from all employers (capped at the Social Security wage base for that year).

**How Benefits Are Calculated**: SSA uses your **35 highest-earning years** (indexed for inflation) to calculate your benefit. If you've worked fewer than 35 years, zeros are averaged in for the missing years — which reduces your benefit. Working additional years with high earnings can replace low-earning years in the calculation.

## Section 3: Your Estimated Benefits — Important Notes

The estimates are based on several assumptions:
- Your future earnings will be the same as your most recent year
- You'll claim at the specified age
- Current law will remain unchanged

These are projections, not guarantees. Social Security is subject to legislative changes.

## Work Credits: Do You Qualify?

To receive retirement benefits, you need **40 work credits** (roughly 10 years of work). You earn up to 4 credits per year based on your earnings ($1,730 per credit in 2024).

Your statement shows your total credits. If you're short, you know exactly how much more you need to work to qualify.

For disability benefits, you need both:
- A minimum number of total credits (based on your age)
- Recent work credits (generally 20 credits in the 10 years before becoming disabled)

## How to Fix Errors

If you find a discrepancy in your earnings record:

1. **Gather documentation**: W-2s, tax returns, pay stubs from the years in question
2. **Contact the SSA**: Call 1-800-772-1213 or visit a local Social Security office
3. **Submit Form SSA-7008**: Request for Correction of Earnings Record
4. **Include evidence**: Provide copies of your W-2s and any employer verification you have

**Time matters**: Correcting records for recent years is easier than for decades-old years, where employment records may no longer exist. Review your statement annually.

## Medicare Enrollment Dates

Your statement includes reminders about Medicare:
- **Part A** (hospital insurance): Free for most people with 40+ work credits. Enroll during your Initial Enrollment Period (7 months around your 65th birthday).
- **Part B** (medical insurance): Requires a monthly premium. Failing to enroll on time results in a permanent 10% penalty per year you delay.

Your statement shows when your Medicare enrollment window opens.
