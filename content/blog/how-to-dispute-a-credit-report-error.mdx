Your credit report affects your mortgage rate, apartment applications, car insurance premiums, and sometimes even job offers. Errors on it are more common than most people realize — and you have a federally-protected right to fix them. Here's how.

## Get Your Free Credit Reports First

You're entitled to one free credit report from each of the three major bureaus — Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion — every week through **AnnualCreditReport.com** (the only federally-mandated free report site).

Pull all three: the same creditor can report differently to each bureau, and an error at one doesn't automatically mean errors at all three.

## What to Look For

Review each report section by section:

### Personal Information

- Correct name, current and past addresses, Social Security number
- Errors here can cause mix-ups with another person's file (especially with common names)

### Accounts / Trade Lines

For each account, verify:

- **Account belongs to you**: Any account you don't recognize could be identity theft or a mixed file
- **Payment history**: Late payments reported incorrectly (you paid on time)
- **Account status**: Open accounts reported as closed (or vice versa), accounts you closed reported as open
- **Balance**: Incorrect balance or credit limit
- **Ownership**: Accounts from a spouse, parent, or ex-partner reported on your report
- **Date of last activity / Date of first delinquency**: These affect how long negatives stay on your report (7 years from first delinquency)

### Collections

- Verify the original creditor, the amount, and the date
- Debts past the statute of limitations in your state can still appear on your report (up to 7 years) but can't be successfully sued

### Public Records

- Bankruptcies: Chapter 7 stays 10 years, Chapter 13 stays 7 years
- Tax liens: Federal tax liens no longer appear on consumer credit reports (removed in 2017–2018)

### Inquiries

- **Hard inquiries** (from applications for credit): Remain 2 years, reduce score slightly
- **Soft inquiries** (from your own checks, pre-approvals): Don't affect score
- Hard inquiries you didn't authorize could indicate fraud

## How to Dispute: Step by Step

### Step 1: Identify the Specific Error

For each error, note:

- The bureau(s) affected
- The creditor name
- The specific incorrect information
- What the correct information should be

### Step 2: Gather Documentation

- Statements showing correct payment dates
- Account closure confirmations
- Bank records confirming payments
- Identity documents if it's a mixed file issue
- Any written communication with the creditor

### Step 3: File Your Dispute

**Online** (fastest): Each bureau has an online dispute portal:

- Equifax: equifax.com/personal/credit-report-services/
- Experian: experian.com/disputes/
- TransUnion: transunion.com/credit-disputes/

**By certified mail** (best paper trail):
Write a dispute letter that includes:

- Your full name and address
- Your SSN and date of birth
- Each item you're disputing, with a clear explanation
- The correction you're requesting
- Copies (not originals) of supporting documents

Send to the credit bureau's dispute address (different from their main address — look it up on their website).

**With the furnisher** (the company that reported the information):
You can also dispute directly with the creditor or collection agency. They are required to investigate and correct or delete inaccurate information.

### Step 4: Track Your Dispute

Each bureau must:

- Investigate your dispute within **30 days** (45 days if you provide additional information during the investigation)
- Forward your dispute and documentation to the furnisher (the company that reported the data)
- Provide you written results of the investigation
- Provide a free copy of your updated report if a change is made

### Step 5: Follow Up If Not Resolved

If the dispute is rejected and you believe the information is still incorrect:

- Submit a second dispute with additional documentation
- Add a **100-word consumer statement** to your file explaining your position
- File a complaint with the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint/
- Contact a consumer protection attorney — the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) allows you to sue and recover damages if bureaus or furnishers fail to follow proper dispute procedures

## How Long Do Disputes Take to Affect Your Score?

- Successful disputes are reflected in your score at the next update cycle
- Removed accounts may improve your score immediately (negative accounts) or have varied effects (old positive accounts)
- Don't apply for new credit during the dispute process — score may fluctuate

## Special Situations

**Identity theft**: Place a fraud alert (free, 1 year) or credit freeze (free) at all three bureaus immediately. A freeze prevents new accounts from being opened in your name.

**Mixed file**: Your file has been mixed with another person's. Often occurs with similar names or SSNs. Request a file separation from the bureau — this may require additional identity documentation.

**Medical collections**: Medical collections under $500 no longer appear on consumer credit reports (as of 2023 — check for updates). If they still appear, dispute them.
