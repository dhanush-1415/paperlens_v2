A wage garnishment is one of the most immediate and disruptive debt collection actions a creditor can take. Once a garnishment order is in place, your employer is legally required to withhold a portion of your paycheck and send it directly to the creditor — before you ever receive your pay.

If you've received a garnishment notice — either from a court or directly from a government agency — here's exactly what it means and what your options are.

## How Wage Garnishment Works

Most creditors (credit card companies, medical providers, landlords) cannot garnish your wages simply because you owe them money. They must first:

1. **Sue you** and win a judgment in court
2. **Receive a writ of garnishment** from the court
3. **Serve your employer** with the garnishment order

Your employer then becomes the "garnishee" — legally obligated to comply. Once served, your employer has very little discretion. They must withhold the required amount from each paycheck and send it to the court or creditor.

**Exception**: Government agencies — including the IRS, state tax authorities, and the Department of Education — can garnish without a court judgment. They have administrative garnishment authority and can act unilaterally.

## Federal Limits on How Much Can Be Taken

The Consumer Credit Protection Act (CCPA) sets federal maximums on garnishment amounts. The rule is: the lesser of:

- **25% of disposable income**, OR
- **The amount by which disposable income exceeds 30 times the federal minimum wage** (currently 30 × $7.25 = $217.50 per week)

"Disposable income" means what's left after legally required deductions — taxes, Social Security, Medicare, and state unemployment insurance. It does not subtract voluntary deductions like health insurance or 401(k).

**Example**: If your disposable income is $600/week:

- 25% = $150
- $600 − $217.50 = $382.50
- The lesser is $150 — so a maximum of $150/week can be garnished

**Example with lower income**: If your disposable income is $300/week:

- 25% = $75
- $300 − $217.50 = $82.50
- The lesser is $75 — maximum garnishment is $75/week

Some states have stricter limits than federal law. When state law is more protective, the state limits apply.

## Child Support and Alimony: Higher Limits

Garnishments for child support and alimony have higher federal limits:

- **Up to 50%** of disposable income if you're supporting another spouse or child
- **Up to 60%** if you're not supporting another family
- **Additional 5%** if more than 12 weeks behind

## IRS Wage Levy: Different Rules

The IRS wage levy is not governed by the CCPA. Instead, the IRS uses Publication 1494 tables to determine an "exempt amount" based on your filing status and number of dependents, and takes everything above that.

For a single filer claiming one personal exemption (standard), the IRS might exempt only a few hundred dollars per paycheck — meaning the IRS can take the vast majority of your pay. This is why IRS levies are so aggressive and why resolving IRS debt through an installment agreement or offer before levy is critical.

An IRS levy also applies to your entire paycheck — not just disposable income. But you can request the exempt amount be adjusted if your actual expenses exceed the table amounts.

## Student Loan Garnishment

Federal student loan holders can garnish up to **15% of disposable income** without a court order through administrative wage garnishment. You must be notified at least 30 days before garnishment begins and have the right to:

- Request a hearing to dispute the debt
- Request a repayment agreement in lieu of garnishment
- Claim financial hardship to reduce or pause the garnishment

Private student loan lenders must obtain a court judgment, like other private creditors.

## How to Stop a Wage Garnishment

### Option 1: Pay the Debt in Full

The most straightforward stop. Once the judgment is satisfied, the creditor releases the garnishment order to your employer.

### Option 2: Negotiate a Settlement or Payment Plan

Many creditors prefer a negotiated arrangement over the expense and time of garnishment. Contact the creditor (or their attorney) directly. Offer a lump sum settlement or a voluntary repayment plan. If they agree, they'll release the garnishment in exchange.

### Option 3: Challenge the Garnishment in Court

You can file a motion to quash (invalidate) the garnishment if:

- The debt isn't yours
- The amount is incorrect
- The creditor didn't follow proper legal procedures
- The statute of limitations on the debt has expired

File your challenge promptly — courts have limited time windows for objections.

### Option 4: Claim an Exemption

Some income is fully exempt from garnishment under federal and state law:

- Social Security benefits (though IRS and child support can still reach them)
- Veterans' benefits
- Federal disability benefits
- Workers' compensation
- In some states: pension income, certain bank account balances

If exempt income has been deposited into your bank account and then frozen by a bank levy, you have the right to claim the exemption and have those funds released.

### Option 5: File for Bankruptcy

Filing for bankruptcy triggers an **automatic stay** — an immediate halt to virtually all collection activity, including garnishments. Chapter 7 can discharge the underlying debt, permanently eliminating the judgment. Chapter 13 can restructure payments over 3–5 years.

**Note**: Bankruptcy doesn't stop child support, alimony, or most tax garnishments — but it can stop credit card, medical, and other consumer debt garnishments immediately.

## Your Employer's Obligations (and Protections for You)

Once served with a garnishment order, your employer:

- Must comply — refusing is contempt of court
- Must not fire you solely because of a single garnishment order (federal law prohibits this; some states extend protection to multiple garnishments)
- Must notify you that the garnishment has been received

Your employer cannot penalize you for the garnishment beyond the required withholding. If you're fired solely because of garnishment, you may have a wrongful termination claim.

## The Notice Before Garnishment Starts

For most court-judgment garnishments, you'll have already been served with the lawsuit and had the opportunity to respond before the garnishment was authorized. But if you missed the lawsuit (this happens when service was improper), you may be able to vacate the default judgment and stop the garnishment before it causes further damage.

For IRS and student loan garnishments, you'll receive advance notice — typically 30 days — before withholding begins. Use that window. It's often your best opportunity to negotiate an alternative arrangement.
