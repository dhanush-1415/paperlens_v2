A power of attorney (POA) is a legal document that gives one person (the "agent" or "attorney-in-fact") the authority to act on behalf of another person (the "principal") in legal, financial, or medical matters. It's one of the foundational documents of estate planning — and one that often arrives in people's lives unexpectedly, either when a loved one becomes incapacitated or when someone needs help managing their affairs.

Understanding what a POA actually authorizes — and what it doesn't — is critical before you sign one, act under one, or rely on one.

## The Four Main Types of Power of Attorney

### 1. General Power of Attorney

A general POA gives your agent broad authority to act on your behalf across virtually all financial and legal matters: buying and selling property, managing bank accounts, handling investments, filing taxes, entering contracts, and more.

**Key limitation**: A general POA is typically "non-durable" by default in many states, meaning it **automatically terminates if the principal becomes incapacitated**. This makes a general POA useful for temporary situations (you're traveling abroad, recovering from surgery) but problematic as an estate planning tool — because it fails exactly when you might need it most.

### 2. Limited (Special) Power of Attorney

A limited POA grants authority for a specific purpose or transaction. Examples:
- Authorizing someone to sell a specific piece of real estate on your behalf
- Giving your accountant authority to deal with the IRS for a specific tax year
- Allowing someone to sign closing documents at a real estate transaction you can't attend

The authority is narrowly defined in the document and expires once the specific purpose is accomplished, or on a specified date.

### 3. Durable Power of Attorney

The word "durable" is the most important concept in POA law. A durable POA **remains in effect even if the principal becomes mentally incapacitated**. This is what most estate planning attorneys recommend.

The durability language is typically explicit — something like: "This power of attorney shall not be affected by the subsequent disability or incapacity of the principal."

Without that language, most states treat a POA as non-durable, meaning it terminates upon incapacity. This is the opposite of what most people intend — they create a POA for the scenario where they become unable to manage their own affairs, but a non-durable POA doesn't work in that scenario.

**If you're creating or reviewing a POA for estate planning purposes, verify it contains explicit durability language.**

### 4. Healthcare Power of Attorney (Medical POA)

A healthcare POA — also called a healthcare proxy or medical power of attorney — specifically authorizes your agent to make medical decisions on your behalf if you're unable to make them yourself.

This is different from a general or durable financial POA. Healthcare POAs:
- Are limited to medical decision-making
- Typically take effect only upon incapacity (your doctor must certify you cannot make your own medical decisions)
- Can authorize decisions about life-sustaining treatment, surgical procedures, care facilities, and end-of-life care (if combined with or supported by a living will/advance directive)

A healthcare POA is separate from a HIPAA authorization, though many people confuse them. A HIPAA release allows someone to receive your health information — a healthcare POA allows someone to make decisions for you.

## The Springing Power of Attorney

A "springing" POA is one that doesn't take effect immediately — it "springs" into effect only when a specific triggering event occurs, usually incapacity as certified by one or two physicians.

The appeal is obvious: the principal retains full control unless and until they truly need help. The problem: the triggering process can cause delays in an emergency. If your agent needs to act immediately but must first obtain physician certifications, there may be a gap in authority precisely when it's most needed.

Many estate planning attorneys recommend an immediately effective durable POA with a strong, trustworthy agent rather than a springing POA.

## When a Power of Attorney Terminates

A POA ends when:

1. **The principal revokes it**: A principal with mental capacity can revoke a POA at any time by signing a written revocation and notifying the agent and any institutions relying on the POA (banks, etc.)
2. **The principal dies**: A POA ceases at death — authority over a deceased person's estate transfers to the executor under a will, not the agent
3. **The purpose is completed**: For a limited POA with a specific purpose
4. **The expiration date arrives**: If the POA includes one
5. **The principal becomes incapacitated**: For non-durable POAs only
6. **Court intervention**: A court can terminate a POA, typically in cases of agent misconduct

**Important**: The death of an agent (attorney-in-fact) terminates the POA unless an alternate agent is named in the document.

## How a POA Differs from Guardianship

People often confuse POA with court-appointed guardianship or conservatorship. Key differences:

- A **POA is created voluntarily** by the principal while they have capacity. It's a private arrangement.
- **Guardianship/conservatorship is court-ordered** — a judge determines that someone lacks capacity and appoints another person to manage their affairs. This is public, expensive, and involves ongoing court supervision.
- A valid durable POA can prevent the need for guardianship. If someone becomes incapacitated without a POA, family members typically must petition the court for guardianship — a process that can take months and cost thousands of dollars.

## What to Do If You've Received a POA Document

**If someone is asking you to sign a POA** (making you a principal granting authority):
- Read the document carefully before signing — understand exactly what authority you're granting
- Verify it contains durability language if you want it to survive incapacity
- Have it notarized (required in most states) and witnessed
- Consider having an attorney review it, especially if it grants broad financial powers

**If you've been named as an agent**:
- You cannot act under the POA until the principal signs it
- Keep the original; make copies for banks, brokerages, or other institutions you'll work with
- Many institutions have their own POA forms they prefer — having both helps
- You have a fiduciary duty to act in the principal's best interest, not your own
- Keep records of every transaction you conduct as agent

**If you've received a POA from someone else (e.g., an agent trying to act on a principal's behalf)**:
- Verify it is signed, dated, and notarized
- Check whether the POA has expired or been revoked
- Banks and financial institutions can refuse POAs that are over a certain age (some refuse documents older than 5 years) — though this practice is being limited by newer state laws
