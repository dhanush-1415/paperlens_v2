A debt collection letter arrives and your first instinct might be to ignore it or panic. Neither is the right move. Understanding your rights under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) puts you in a much stronger position — regardless of whether you actually owe the debt.

## What Is the FDCPA?

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act is a federal law that regulates how third-party debt collectors can contact you and what they can say. It covers:
- Collection agencies (not original creditors in most cases)
- Attorneys collecting debt on behalf of clients
- Debt buyers who purchased your debt from the original creditor

**Note**: The FDCPA applies to consumer debts (credit cards, medical bills, auto loans, mortgages) — not business debts.

## What Every Initial Collection Letter Must Include

Within 5 days of first contacting you, a debt collector must send you a written notice including:
1. The amount of the debt
2. The name of the original creditor
3. A statement that you have 30 days to dispute the debt
4. Notice that if you don't dispute within 30 days, the debt is assumed valid
5. Notice that if you dispute the debt, the collector will provide verification

If the letter you received doesn't include these items, the collector may be violating the FDCPA.

## The Debt Validation Letter — Your Most Powerful Tool

Within **30 days** of receiving a collection letter, you can send a **debt validation request** by certified mail. This requires the collector to:
- Provide verification of the original debt
- Provide the original creditor's name and address
- Stop all collection activity until they provide verification

If they can't verify the debt, they must stop collecting. Many old, purchased debts cannot be verified — and collectors know this.

**Template request to include**:
> "I am writing to request validation of this debt pursuant to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1692g. Please provide: the amount of the debt, the name of the original creditor, a copy of the original agreement or documentation that I owe this debt, and confirmation that your agency is licensed to collect in my state."

Send it within 30 days. Keep the certified mail receipt.

## What Collectors Are Prohibited From Doing

Under the FDCPA, debt collectors **cannot**:
- Call before 8 AM or after 9 PM
- Call you at work if you've told them your employer doesn't allow it
- Use threatening, abusive, or obscene language
- Make false statements (claim to be attorneys, government officials, etc.)
- Threaten arrest for unpaid debt (civil debt is not a criminal matter)
- Garnish wages or sue without actually doing so
- Contact third parties (family, neighbors, employers) about your debt — except to locate you
- Continue contacting you after you send a written cease communication request

## How to Read a Collection Letter

**Check the creditor**: Is it a name you recognize? Does it match a debt you know you have?

**Check the amount**: Does it match what you believe you owe? Collection fees and interest are sometimes added — confirm these are legally permitted.

**Check the date**: There's a statute of limitations on debt — typically 3–7 years depending on your state and debt type. After this period, collectors can still try to collect but generally can't sue you successfully. Paying on old debt can restart the clock.

**Check the collector's license**: In most states, collection agencies must be licensed to operate. You can verify their license with your state's financial regulatory agency.

## The Statute of Limitations — Know Your State

| State | SOL (Written Contract) |
|-------|----------------------|
| California | 4 years |
| New York | 6 years |
| Texas | 4 years |
| Florida | 5 years |
| Illinois | 5 years |
| Ohio | 6 years |

**Important**: If a debt is past the statute of limitations, the collector cannot successfully sue you — but they can still contact you. Acknowledge this and consider your options carefully before paying old debt.

## Your Options

### Pay in Full
If you owe the debt and it's within the statute of limitations, paying it eliminates the obligation. Get a written payoff letter before paying.

### Settle for Less
Collectors often buy old debt for pennies on the dollar and have room to settle. Offer 25–50% of the amount owed in writing, contingent on a written settlement agreement and a "paid in full" notation with credit bureaus.

### Request Cease of Communication
You can write to the collector (certified mail) requesting they stop contacting you. They can contact you once more after that to confirm they're stopping or to notify you of an action. This doesn't eliminate the debt — they can still sue.

### Dispute the Debt
If you don't believe you owe it, or the amount is wrong, dispute it in writing within 30 days. If you've already missed the 30-day window, you can still dispute — collection activity just doesn't have to pause while they investigate.

## If You Believe Collectors Are Violating the FDCPA

Document every contact. Note dates, times, names, and what was said. You can:
- File a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) at consumerfinance.gov
- File a complaint with the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov
- Contact your state attorney general's office
- Sue the collector — the FDCPA allows you to recover damages, and attorneys often take these cases on contingency
