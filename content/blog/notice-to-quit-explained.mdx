You received a notice from your landlord. Maybe it's called a Notice to Quit. Maybe it says Notice to Vacate. Maybe it says "Pay or Quit" or "Cure or Quit." These names are not interchangeable — they each represent a different stage in the eviction process with different legal consequences and different response options. Here's exactly what each one means.

## Notice to Quit vs. Notice to Vacate: The Core Difference

### Notice to Quit
A Notice to Quit is a demand that you either **fix a problem or leave**. It is the first legal step in most eviction processes. The "quit" refers to either quitting the behavior causing the problem or quitting (vacating) the premises if you don't fix it.

There are three types:

**Pay or Quit**: You owe rent. Pay the full past-due amount within the notice period or vacate.

**Cure or Quit**: You violated a term of your lease (unauthorized pet, too many occupants, property damage). Fix the violation within the notice period or vacate.

**Unconditional Quit**: You have committed a serious violation — typically repeated violations, significant property damage, illegal activity on the premises, or non-payment of rent for the second time in a short period. You must leave. There is no option to fix anything.

### Notice to Vacate
A Notice to Vacate is a demand that you leave the premises — **regardless of whether you've done anything wrong**. It typically arises when:
- Your lease has expired and the landlord doesn't want to renew
- You are a month-to-month tenant and the landlord is terminating the tenancy
- The landlord is selling the property and needs it vacant
- The landlord is moving into the property themselves (owner move-in)

A Notice to Vacate doesn't accuse you of wrongdoing. It simply ends the tenancy and requires you to leave by the specified date.

## Notice Periods: What the Timeframe Means

The notice period determines how much time you have before the landlord can take legal action. These periods are set by state law and sometimes local ordinance:

### 3-Day Notice
Most common for Pay or Quit situations. You have three days from the date of service (not counting weekends or holidays in some states) to pay or leave. Three days is a short window — act immediately if you receive one.

### 5-Day Notice
Used in some states as the pay-or-quit period. Same urgency as 3-day.

### 10-Day or 14-Day Notice
Often used for lease violations (cure or quit) — you have more time to fix the problem. Read the notice carefully to understand what you're required to cure.

### 30-Day Notice
Most common for month-to-month tenancy termination when neither party is at fault. You have 30 days from service to vacate. In some states, landlords must give more notice for tenants who have lived there longer.

### 60-Day Notice
Many states require 60 days' notice for tenants who have occupied a rental for more than 1 year. California requires 60 days for month-to-month tenants of one year or more. Check your state's specific rules.

## What "Pay or Quit" Actually Means

A "Pay or Quit" notice requires you to pay the **full amount stated** — not a partial payment, not a promise to pay — within the notice period. In most states:
- Paying the full amount in time stops the eviction process
- A partial payment may not stop the eviction (check your state's law — some states require landlords to accept partial payment)
- Paying after the notice period has expired does not automatically stop the eviction — the landlord may choose to accept it or proceed anyway

If you receive a Pay or Quit notice, get the funds together or explore your options immediately. Call 211 (United Way's helpline) to find emergency rental assistance programs in your area.

## Your Options When You Receive Any Notice

### Option 1: Comply
Pay what you owe, cure the violation, or vacate by the deadline. This ends the matter without a court record against you.

### Option 2: Negotiate
Contact your landlord in writing and propose a solution — a payment plan for back rent, a timeline to cure a violation, a later move-out date if you need time. Get any agreement in writing. Landlords often prefer a negotiated resolution over the time and expense of eviction court.

### Option 3: Assert a Defense
You may have legal defenses that stop or delay the eviction:
- **Improper service**: The notice wasn't served according to your state's requirements (wrong delivery method, wrong person served)
- **Retaliatory eviction**: The landlord filed in response to you complaining about habitability issues or contacting code enforcement
- **Discriminatory eviction**: The notice is based on your protected class (race, religion, disability, familial status, etc.)
- **Rent was tendered and refused**: You tried to pay and the landlord refused — document this
- **Habitability issues**: You may be able to withhold rent or offset rent if the landlord failed to maintain the property in habitable condition (state-specific)

### Option 4: Wait and Contest at Court
If the landlord files for eviction (unlawful detainer), you have the right to respond and have a hearing. But doing nothing and forcing the landlord to file is not the same as having a defense — it just delays the timeline.

## What Happens If You Do Nothing

If you ignore the notice and don't pay, cure, or vacate:

1. The landlord files an **unlawful detainer** (eviction) lawsuit with the local court
2. You are served with a summons and complaint — you have a short time to respond (often 5 days)
3. If you don't respond, the court enters a **default judgment** against you
4. The court issues a **writ of possession** authorizing the sheriff or marshal to remove you
5. You are physically removed from the property on the date set by the writ
6. An eviction judgment appears on your **rental history** and can make it very difficult to rent again for years

This entire process can happen in as little as 2–4 weeks in some states. Doing nothing is the worst option.

## How to Request More Time

If you need more time to move out and have no legal defense, be honest with your landlord. Many landlords will grant a short extension (7–14 days) in exchange for:
- A written agreement to vacate by the new date
- Payment of additional rent or fees
- A confirmed departure date that avoids eviction court costs

Get any extension agreement in writing, signed by both parties.
