You open a letter from an attorney's office. It's addressed to you. It says "DEMAND" at the top and mentions a specific dollar amount. Your heart rate jumps. Before you panic, let's clarify what a demand letter actually is — and what it can legally do.

## What Is a Demand Letter?

A demand letter is a **formal written request** from one party to another asking them to take a specific action — usually to pay money, stop doing something, or fulfill a contract obligation. It is a **pre-litigation document**, meaning it's what happens before a lawsuit, not the lawsuit itself.

Demand letters are sent by:

- Attorneys on behalf of their clients
- Businesses collecting unpaid invoices
- Landlords demanding unpaid rent
- Individuals seeking personal injury settlements
- Creditors before filing suit
- Intellectual property holders demanding you stop using their material

## What a Demand Letter Is NOT

- **Not a court order** — you are not legally required to comply with a demand letter
- **Not a lawsuit** — receiving a demand letter does not mean you've been sued
- **Not an automatic judgment** — no money is owed to the sender unless a court decides otherwise (or you agree)
- **Not a guarantee of legal action** — many demand letters are bluffs; many senders never file suit

## Anatomy of a Demand Letter

A typical demand letter contains:

1. **Sender identification** — who is making the demand (attorney, company, individual)
2. **Your identification** — confirming it's addressed to you
3. **Statement of facts** — their version of what happened
4. **Legal basis** — what law or contract they're relying on
5. **The demand** — what they want (usually a specific dollar amount)
6. **Deadline** — how long you have to respond (commonly 7–30 days)
7. **Consequences** — what happens if you don't comply (lawsuit, reporting to credit bureaus, etc.)

## How Serious Is It?

Seriousness depends on the source and the claim:

| Type                                 | Typical Urgency                       |
| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------- |
| Attorney demand for personal injury  | High — litigation is genuinely likely |
| Business unpaid invoice demand       | Medium — often negotiable             |
| Debt collection agency demand        | Medium — subject to FDCPA rules       |
| Cease and desist for online content  | Varies — often not filed in court     |
| Small-claims style individual demand | Lower — may be posturing              |

An attorney demand letter with specific case citations and a realistic dollar amount tends to be more serious than a boilerplate collection letter.

## What to Do When You Receive One

### Step 1: Don't Ignore It

Even if you believe the claim is baseless, ignoring a demand letter typically results in the sender filing suit. It's almost always better to respond.

### Step 2: Read It Carefully

Identify:

- What exactly is being demanded (amount, action, or cessation)
- The deadline for response
- The legal basis for the claim
- Whether the claim has any merit from your perspective

### Step 3: Assess Your Situation

- Do you owe the money or have the obligation they're claiming?
- Is the amount accurate?
- Do you have documentation that contradicts their version of events?
- Is this a case where negotiation makes sense?

### Step 4: Respond (Or Get Help)

For small, clear-cut matters, you can respond yourself — in writing, addressing their claims factually and professionally. For anything involving more than a few thousand dollars or complex legal issues, consult an attorney.

**Never ignore a deadline without communicating.** If you need more time, write to the sender asking for an extension. Most attorneys will grant one.

## Responding to a Demand Letter

A response to a demand letter should:

- Acknowledge receipt without admitting any wrongdoing
- State your position on their claims
- Request documentation they claim to have
- Propose a resolution if appropriate
- Be in writing (email with read receipt or certified mail)

**Do not** make statements that could be used against you in court. Do not admit liability unless you're prepared to pay. Do not agree to a settlement amount in writing without understanding the full release of claims.

## Common Types and How to Handle Them

**Debt collection demand**: Verify the debt within 30 days using a debt validation letter. The creditor must prove you owe the amount claimed.

**Unpaid rent**: If you owe it, pay or negotiate a plan. If you don't, document the discrepancy in writing.

**Personal injury**: Do not respond without an attorney if there's potential liability. What you say can be used against you.

**Copyright/trademark cease and desist**: Consult an IP attorney before responding. Sometimes the claim is meritless; sometimes it's serious.

**Contract dispute**: Review the contract and compare their claims to what the agreement actually says.
