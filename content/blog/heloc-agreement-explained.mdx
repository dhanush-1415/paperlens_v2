A home equity line of credit sounds like a convenient financial tool — borrow what you need, pay interest only on what you use, pay it back on your own schedule. And it can be those things. But a HELOC is secured by your home, and the fine print in the agreement determines how much risk you're actually taking on. Here's what every major section means before you sign.

## What a HELOC Is (and Isn't)

A HELOC is a **revolving line of credit** secured by the equity in your home. Like a credit card, you can draw funds, repay them, and draw again — up to your credit limit. Unlike a credit card, your home is the collateral. If you default, the lender can foreclose.

A HELOC is different from a home equity loan (sometimes called a "second mortgage"):

| Feature | HELOC | Home Equity Loan |
|---------|-------|-----------------|
| Structure | Revolving line of credit | Lump sum |
| Interest rate | Variable (usually) | Fixed |
| Payment structure | Interest-only during draw | Principal + interest |
| Flexibility | Draw as needed | Single disbursement |
| Risk | Rate can increase significantly | Payment predictable |

## The Draw Period vs. the Repayment Period

This is the structural feature most people don't fully understand when they sign.

### The Draw Period
Typically **5–10 years**. During this time:
- You can borrow up to your credit limit at any time
- Payments are usually **interest-only** on the outstanding balance
- Your minimum payment is low — sometimes very low
- You can repay and re-borrow freely

The interest-only structure during the draw period feels manageable. The problem: you're not reducing principal. The full amount you've borrowed is still owed when the draw period ends.

### The Repayment Period
Typically **10–20 years** after the draw period ends. During this time:
- The line of credit closes — you can no longer draw funds
- Payments shift to **principal + interest**, fully amortized over the repayment period
- Your monthly payment can jump dramatically — sometimes by 50–100%

Example: You have a $100,000 HELOC balance when the draw period ends. At 8% interest, interest-only payments were $667/month. When repayment begins over 15 years, your payment jumps to approximately $956/month — and that's before accounting for any rate increases.

This payment shock catches many HELOC holders off guard. Know when your draw period ends and plan for the transition.

## Variable Rate Risk: The Prime Rate Connection

Almost all HELOCs carry **variable interest rates** tied to the **Prime Rate** (currently tied to the Federal Reserve's benchmark rate). Your agreement will state something like "Prime Rate + 1%" or "Prime Rate + 0.5%."

What this means in practice:
- When the Fed raises rates, your HELOC rate rises immediately in most cases
- A HELOC at Prime + 1% when Prime is 5% costs you 6% — but if Prime rises to 8%, you're paying 9%
- There's usually a lifetime interest rate cap (common caps are 18% or 25%) — verify this in your agreement
- There's usually a floor rate below which your rate cannot fall

During periods of rising interest rates, HELOC borrowers can see their monthly payments increase significantly with no action on their part. Model out what your payment looks like if rates rise 3–5 percentage points.

## Credit Limit Changes: When the Lender Reduces Your Line

This is one of the most alarming clauses in a HELOC agreement — and many borrowers don't discover it until it happens to them.

Your lender can **reduce or suspend your HELOC** if:
- Your home's value declines and your equity drops below the lender's threshold
- You miss payments or your creditworthiness deteriorates
- The lender determines you are significantly overextended
- There is a "material adverse change" in your financial situation

This happened to thousands of borrowers during the 2008 housing crisis. Lenders froze or canceled HELOCs as home values declined. Borrowers who had been counting on access to that credit line found it suddenly unavailable.

If you're using a HELOC as a financial safety net, understand that the lender can remove that safety net — precisely when you're most likely to need it (during a financial downturn that also depresses home values).

## The Interest-Only Trap During the Draw Period

Paying interest only during the draw period keeps your payment low — but it can create a behavioral trap. Many borrowers:
1. Draw funds freely because the payment feels affordable
2. Carry a large balance throughout the entire draw period
3. Are shocked when principal payments begin in the repayment period
4. Are unable to afford the higher payments or refinance because of their financial situation

The discipline required: treat your HELOC like a loan, not a credit card. Pay principal voluntarily during the draw period so you enter repayment with a manageable balance.

## What Happens if You Default

A HELOC is secured by your home. If you stop making payments:

1. The lender will report the delinquency to credit bureaus
2. After sufficient delinquency (varies by lender and state), the lender can begin foreclosure proceedings
3. If you have a first mortgage with a different lender, a HELOC foreclosure gets complicated — the HELOC is typically a junior lien, which means the first mortgage lender is paid first from any sale proceeds

Even if foreclosure doesn't occur, a delinquent HELOC will damage your credit score and trigger the credit limit reduction provisions described above.

## The Acceleration Clause

Most HELOC agreements contain an **acceleration clause**: if you default, the entire outstanding balance (not just the missed payments) becomes immediately due in full. This is the legal mechanism that can trigger foreclosure — the lender calls the full loan, you can't pay it in full, and the collateral (your home) is at risk.

Acceleration clauses are standard across virtually all secured lending agreements. But seeing it in your HELOC agreement is a reminder that the stakes of default are not just a missed payment — they're the entire loan.

## HELOC vs. Home Equity Loan: Which Is Right for You

**Choose a HELOC if:**
- You need flexible access to funds over time (home improvement project in phases, business investment)
- You can tolerate variable rate risk
- You have the discipline to manage a revolving credit line

**Choose a home equity loan if:**
- You need a specific lump sum for a defined purpose
- You want payment predictability
- You're concerned about rate increases
- The interest-only period trap is a risk for your situation

## Before You Sign

Read the sections on:
- **Rate calculation**: Exact formula for your interest rate
- **Rate caps and floors**: Maximum rate you can ever be charged
- **Draw period end date**: When does the line close?
- **Repayment period length**: How long do you have to repay?
- **Suspension and reduction rights**: Under what conditions can the lender reduce your line?
- **Acceleration clause**: What triggers it and what are the timeframes?
